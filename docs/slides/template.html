The clinical problem

Background

Stroke is among the leading causes of long-term disability worldwide. Cognitive impairment affects roughly one in three survivors, persisting beyond the acute phase across attention, memory, and executive function.

Dozens of cognitive interventions have been trialled — from computerised training to compensatory strategy programmes — yet the evidence base remains fragmented and difficult to synthesise.

Why meta-research

Approach

A meta-research perspective steps back from individual trials and asks how the field as a whole is conducting, reporting, and synthesising evidence.

It treats the literature itself as the data — and applies the same methodological rigour to it that we would apply to any primary study.

Three questions

  1. How are these trials designed?
  2. What is reported, and how?
  3. How can the evidence be reliably synthesised?

Part two · methods

Systematic review protocol

Inclusion · sources

Inclusion

RCTs of post-stroke cognitive interventions; adult survivors with documented cognitive impairment at baseline; follow-up of at least four weeks.

Part three · findings

Pooled effect on global cognition

Primary outcome

0.31

SMD, 95% CI 0.18 – 0.44 · k = 47 trials · n = 4,218 participants

Substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68%) suggests effects are not uniform across intervention types, populations, or follow-up windows.

On the methodology itself

The field has produced dozens of trials, but their methodological diversity — small samples, inconsistent outcome measures, brief follow-up — makes a confident pooled estimate elusive without careful sub-group analysis.

— Discussion, Chapter 5

Sub-group analysis

Intervention modality

  • Computerised training · 0.42 (95% CI 0.24 – 0.60), k = 18
  • Compensatory strategy · 0.28 (95% CI 0.09 – 0.47), k = 14
  • Combined / multi-modal · 0.21 (95% CI 0.04 – 0.38), k = 10
  • Other / unclassified · 0.15 (95% CI -0.08 – 0.38), k = 5

Effects largest for computerised training, but with greatest heterogeneity — caution warranted.

Part four · discussion

Open questions

Next steps

  1. Which intervention modalities show the most consistent effects across populations?
  2. Are there patient-level moderators — stroke type, time-since-onset, baseline severity — that predict response?
  3. How should future trials be designed to support meaningful evidence synthesis?

Implications for the field

What needs to change

The cognitive rehabilitation literature would benefit from:

  • Standardised outcome sets — a minimum reporting standard for trial endpoints
  • Pre-registration as routine practice, not exception
  • Patient-level data sharing to enable individual-participant meta-analysis
  • Longer follow-up to test whether short-term gains translate into durable functional change
